Waves into vision.

A helmet of ultrasound sensors can see a stroke through the skull, cheaply and without radiation. It takes 32 hours of computing to turn the sound into an image. We broke that computation apart and tested what a quantum computer can and cannot do for it.

The problem

No stroke can be treated until someone sees inside the head.

A clot-busting drug saves a brain with a clot and worsens a brain with a bleed. Guidelines require imaging first, and the scanners live in hospitals.

795Kstrokes a year in the USAHA 2026
1.9Mneurons lost per untreated minuteSaver, Stroke 2006
113MAmericans without 60-min access to clot removalJ NeuroIntervent Surg 2022
$56.2BUS cost of stroke each yearCDC

87% are clots

Treated with clot-busting drugs within 4.5 hours, or clot removal within 24.

13% are bleeds

The same drug makes them worse. “Brain imaging is essential to exclude hemorrhage.” (AHA/ASA 2026)

The breakthrough

Seeing through the skull with sound.

Researchers at Imperial College London showed that a ring of ultrasound sensors and a method borrowed from seismic imaging can map the brain through bone (Guasch et al., npj Digital Medicine, 2020).

01 · THE HELMET

1,024 sensors around the head

Each transducer is both a speaker and a microphone. No radiation, no magnet, small enough to imagine in an ambulance.

02 · FIRE AND LISTEN

One speaks, all listen

Each sensor fires a short pulse in turn. Sound speeds up in skull (2,814 m/s) and slows in brain (about 1,505 m/s). Those changes are the image. About a million recordings in seconds.

03 · COMPUTE

Guess, simulate, compare, adjust

Full-waveform inversion guesses the head, simulates the sound through it, compares with the recordings, and corrects the guess. About 80 rounds, from blurry to sharp.

04 · RESULT

In simulation, it found the bleed

The method recovered a haemorrhage in a simulated head. No in-vivo brain image has been published yet, and one 3D image took 32 hours.

The catch

A beautiful device hospitals can’t afford to use.

32 h

for one 3D image on 128 CPU nodes × 24 cores.

~$1.2M

of GPU servers the authors say would bring it under 10 minutes.

>95%

of that compute is one step: simulating the sound wave.

Our approach

Find the slow step. Test the quantum route. Keep the rest classical.

The wave simulation conserves energy, the same rule a quantum computer obeys, so it can be rewritten as a quantum circuit. Reading results out is expensive, so the quantum part is asked for one number only.

The quantum part

The wave becomes a packet hopping along a chain of qubits.

Each cell holds velocity and each link between cells holds stretch. Their squared amplitudes are shares of the wave’s energy, so they always add to one, just like quantum probability. Below: the team’s 7-cell ear-to-ear line, exactly as their circuits run it.

Time to answer

Today, classical wins by a mile.

Log scale. Dark bars are classical, light bars quantum.

Where quantum could still win: per time step, classical work grows with every cell; a fault-tolerant compact circuit grows roughly with the logarithm of the cell count. The best published net gain is polynomial, at best quartic in 3D, and only when one number is read out (Bösch et al., Phys. Rev. Research 2025).

Limits

What we can claim, and what we can’t.

We showed

  • The wave simulation runs as a quantum circuit and matches classical
  • Error mitigation recovers the signal on an IBM noise model
  • Our classical reconstruction finds a bleed
  • What a full head would need: 29 qubits, 10¹³ gates, 10¹³ shots

We don’t claim

  • Any speed-up over GPUs today
  • That a quantum run detected the bleed
  • Any clinical result
  • Realism: no absorption, toy grids, energy rather than pressure
