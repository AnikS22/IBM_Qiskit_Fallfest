aperture

ap·er·ture /ˈapərCHər/ noun 1. an opening through which imaging signals pass. 2. the size of that opening, which sets how much you can see.

Opening access to medical diagnostics.

The idea

Sound through the skull.

Guasch et al. (2020) proposed a helmet of 1,024 ultrasound transducers that could ride in an ambulance. Each one fires, the rest listen, and full-waveform inversion rebuilds the brain from how sound slowed down on the way through the skull. Our logo is that idea in one line: a pulse passing through the skull. The opening carries three meanings.

01 · Signal

A way through the skull

Ultrasound normally can't see past bone. The paper's algorithm opens that aperture.

02 · Access

The scan comes to the patient

A stroke scan in the ambulance, not an hour later in the hospital.

03 · Research

An open question

Sound waves conserve energy the way qubits do. We tested whether that makes the simulation quantum-shaped, and measured where it falls short.

Story architecture

Seven acts, one patient.

The deck follows your order. Each act lists the rubric line it is built to win.

1

The big problem

A stroke patient in an ambulance. Clot or bleed? The clot-busting drug that saves one worsens the other, so doctors must see first. CT and MRI stay in the hospital, and every minute of waiting kills brain tissue.

Problem 15%Presentation 15%
2

Current solutions & the new tech

CT and MRI are big, fixed and slow to reach. Ultrasound tomography is portable, cheap and radiation-free, and it is going commercial (Midjourney Medical's Ultrasonic CT, 2026). Guasch et al. propose a 1,024-transducer helmet for the ambulance.

Problem 15%Innovation 10%
3

The caveat

Ultrasound can't see through skull. Full-waveform inversion gets around it by guessing the head, simulating sound through it, and correcting, over and over. The simulation step is the cost: 85 million voxels, 1,024 sources, 32 hours on 128 computers.

Guasch et al., "Full-waveform inversion imaging of the human brain," npj Digital Medicine 3, 28 (2020). doi:10.1038/s41746-020-0240-8

Quantum Rationale 20%Problem 15%
4

The question

Sound conserves energy the way a quantum computer conserves its total, so the wave equation can be rewritten as a quantum circuit. 29 qubits could hold the state of the whole head. Could quantum run the expensive step?

Costa, Jordan & Ostrander, "Quantum algorithm for simulating the wave equation," Phys. Rev. A 99, 012323 (2019)

Quantum Rationale 20%Innovation 10%
5

Comparison studies

One line of 7 cubes from ear to ear: scalp, skull, brain, skull, scalp. Classical finite-difference simulation vs two quantum encodings: compact (4 qubits, ~1,100 two-qubit gates) and one-qubit-per-cube (15 qubits, ~240 gates). Metric fixed before the run: signal arriving at the microphone cube.

Results 10%Quantum Rationale 20%
6

The experiments

Statevector matches classical. Then real IBM hardware: raw vs error-mitigated, repeated runs with error bars. Show how much noise destroys and how much mitigation recovers. Echo for classical, Pulse for quantum.

Technical 20%Hardware +5
7

Implementation & what comes next

The wall is readout: FWI needs about a billion numbers per round, and a quantum computer gives one click per shot. So the design is hybrid. Quantum estimates a single number (total mismatch, or bleed yes/no) and classical does the rest. Resource estimate for 3D: ~29 qubits, millions of error-free gates, so this needs error-corrected machines. Close on the ambulance.
